The town-hall meeting in August was for the people who teach. The next step was for the
people who are about to learn. Before term began, Anna Sigríður Islind and I met the
incoming students of every department at Reykjavik University, the college-preparation
program included, about 700 in all, for ninety minutes on AI and learning.

We told them at the start what the hour was not: not a set of rules, and not a sales
pitch. They are the first cohort to go through a whole degree with this tool beside them,
and the university has no complete answer to offer them, because nobody does. What we
could offer was one idea to hold on to. AI multiplies what you can do, but it must not take
over your judgment. A bike or a taxi will both get you there, and only one of them builds your
legs.

Most of the hour was spent doing rather than listening. They watched a model invent a
citation and then cave when pushed on a correct answer, and learned four quick ways to
check. They spent twenty minutes with an AI tutor on their own phones, on a first-year
topic they thought they already knew. The tutor was set up to quiz them first and explain
only afterwards, so the twenty minutes tested what they actually knew rather than
handing them a summary. And they placed themselves on a ladder of five stances toward AI, from
consumer to professional, where each step up moves more of the responsibility onto the
person holding the phone.

We left the advanced material out on purpose. Directing tools and building things can
wait; owning your judgment comes first.

Whether an hour changes anything, we cannot tell yet. What it does is make sure that no
student at the university can say, a year from now, that nobody told them the difference
between finishing the work and digesting it.
